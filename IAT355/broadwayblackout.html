How has the popularity of Broadway shows shifted across 1900-2020?

Broadway saw a surge in the volume of titles opened in the 1900s and achieved its all time peak during the 1920s, before meeting a sharp decline in the subsequent decades.

The surge in openings during the 1900s can be partly attributed to a wave of new theaters being built in the Broadway district, including the Republic, the Lyric, New Amsterdam and the Lew Fields.

After a resurgence in the 1980s, the numbers settled into a stable period until 2020, which brought the amount of titles opened to an unprecedented zero

The Golden Age of Broadway

The golden age of Broadway lines up with the post World War I economic boom known as the Roaring Twenties (Cunningham). Rising incomes and technological growth gave Americans more time and money for leisure, and Broadway was a direct beneficiary, with iconic productions like Chicago and the Great Gasby, cementing this district as a cultural centerpiece of the decade (Broadway Inbound).

The 1920s and Onwards

The sharp decline in openings following the 1920s peak aligns directly with the Great Depression, which spanned from 1929 to 1939. Widespread unemployment and collapsing household incomes left little room for leisure spending, cutting into both audience demand and the financial viability of producing new shows. The result was a decade-long contraction that effectively reversed Broadway's golden era growth.

The musical is widely regarded as a "quintessentially American art form" (Hodge 2020, p.5), born from an amalgamation of British, French, and varied popular entertainment traditions. Its dominance on Broadway reflects a broader cultural identity, as Wolf (2002) argues that the language of Broadway is the language of America, a stage where the country's highest artistic ambitions are performed and preserved.

It is this cultural significance that has sustained the musical and the play as Broadway's two defining show types across two centuries.

Burlesque and The Death of A Genre

Burlesque's near-disappearance from Broadway is largely attributed to Mayor Fiorello H. La Guardia's legislative crackdown in the late 1940s (Caldwell), which targeted the genre's sexually provocative performances and shut down the venues that hosted them.

Combined with stricter regulations on public alcohol consumption, these measures effectively pushed Burlesque off Broadway by the 1940s. A brief revival emerged in non-Broadway venues in the early 1990s, though it never regained a foothold in the mainstream.

...So what about Opera?
Was Timothee Chalamet right?

Opera's limited presence on Broadway relative to other show types stems from two structural barriers. First, the genre carries formal audience expectations rooted in classical convention, demanding a level of cultural familiarity that musical theatre does not require.

Second, many canonical operas are performed in Italian, German, or French, limiting accessibility for the predominantly English-speaking Broadway audience. Together, these factors have historically kept opera from competing for mainstream Broadway programming, confining it to a small and consistent share of productions across the dataset's two-century span.

Musicals: Dominance of Original vs. Revival

Original musicals have continued to dominate Broadway programming throughout the 20th and 21st centuries, a trend shaped as much by financial calculation as artistic ambition.

Turn of the Century

The high number of original musicals in the early 20th century reflects how new the genre was at the time. The musical as an art form is commonly traced back to the 1866 production of The Black Crook (Dance Informa Magazine), meaning the genre had only around three decades of history by the turn of the century. With such a short history, there was little existing material to revive, so original productions dominated Broadway's programming as writers and composers worked to build and define the genre.
